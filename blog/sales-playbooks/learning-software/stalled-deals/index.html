Review a stalled opportunity for learning software

The Prospecting Show with Dr. Connor Robertson · Editorial sales resource · October 12, 2026

Find whether the deal is waiting on a decision, missing evidence, or no longer relevant. This guide applies the workflow to selling learning software, where a useful conversation may involve the learning manager and department leader and a practical review of training completion detached from practical skill.

The finished result is a stalled-opportunity review. Work from information the buyer confirms. The examples below are planning models; replace bracketed fields and unsupported assumptions before using them with an actual account.

Start with the buyer’s operating context

For this selling context, a possible reason to investigate involves a new training program. That event alone does not establish demand. Check whether the team experiences this issue: training completion detached from practical skill. Confirm who owns the workflow and whether a change is worth evaluating.

A practical sequence

  1. Review the last buyer-confirmed commitment.
  2. Identify the unanswered question or blocked action.
  3. Ask whether the original priority still exists.
  4. Agree to a dated next step, a nurture plan, or closure.

Apply these steps to the same account rather than changing the hypothesis halfway through the exercise. If new evidence contradicts your starting assumption, update the record and reconsider the next action. The objective is a useful decision, not a completed form.

Worked example: review a stalled opportunity

Illustrative example, not a report of a client result.

Check whether the buyer ever committed to reviewing completion, practice evidence, and manager follow-through. Then ask: “Is training completion detached from practical skill still a priority, or has the review changed?” Offer to agree on a demo linked to a specific learner task, set a buyer-requested revisit condition, or close the opportunity.

Use a concrete artifact

A useful supporting resource in this context is a learning-outcomes worksheet. It should help the learning manager and department leader assess the current situation without forcing them through a generic sales presentation. Keep the scope narrow enough that the buyer can correct it.

For example, connect the artifact to completion, practice evidence, and manager follow-through. Identify which inputs came from the buyer, which are public observations, and which remain assumptions. Do not request private operational records before the buyer has agreed an appropriate purpose and handling process.

Evaluate the result

Track stalled records resolved into a clear disposition. Define the numerator, denominator, and review window before comparing results. Use comparable accounts and channels; otherwise an apparent improvement may simply reflect a different list or buying context.

For learning software, pair the workflow measure with the quality of evidence about training completion detached from practical skill. A reply or meeting is useful only if it helps establish relevance. Record declined and unsuitable opportunities so the team can improve targeting as well as messaging.

A common failure to avoid

Do not treat silence as proof that a deal is still active. Moving a close date without new evidence weakens the forecast.

In this context, the practical consequence is spending time on a demo linked to a specific learner task before the buyer has confirmed a reason to do it. Revisit content migration, accessibility, and reporting needs early enough to discover a genuine mismatch.

When to move forward

Proceed only when a buyer-owned action exists or the record is deliberately reclassified. A sensible next step may be a demo linked to a specific learner task, but only when it follows from the evidence. If the buyer cannot identify a relevant problem or owner, close or reclassify the record instead of manufacturing urgency.
