Demonstrate a solution for product design services

The Prospecting Show with Dr. Connor Robertson · Editorial sales resource · October 12, 2026

Show how a specific buyer task could work and identify gaps honestly. This guide applies the workflow to selling product design services, where a useful conversation may involve the product manager and design leader and a practical review of a user workflow remaining unclear after feature discussions.

The finished result is a workflow demo plan. Work from information the buyer confirms. The examples below are planning models; replace bracketed fields and unsupported assumptions before using them with an actual account.

Start with the buyer’s operating context

For this selling context, a possible reason to investigate involves a product release or workflow redesign. That event alone does not establish demand. Check whether the team experiences this issue: a user workflow remaining unclear after feature discussions. Confirm who owns the workflow and whether a change is worth evaluating.

A practical sequence

  1. Choose a workflow confirmed during discovery.
  2. Use sample information rather than sensitive customer records.
  3. Show the relevant task from input to outcome.
  4. Ask the buyer to identify differences from their actual process.

Apply these steps to the same account rather than changing the hypothesis halfway through the exercise. If new evidence contradicts your starting assumption, update the record and reconsider the next action. The objective is a useful decision, not a completed form.

Worked example: demonstrate a solution

Illustrative example, not a report of a client result.

Use a fictional case involving a user workflow remaining unclear after feature discussions. Show the relevant inputs, action, exception, and output. Ask the product manager and design leader whether the demonstration reflects the actual workflow, especially user access, prototype scope, and engineering dependencies. Document the differences rather than claiming full fit.

Use a concrete artifact

A useful supporting resource in this context is a task-based usability brief. It should help the product manager and design leader assess the current situation without forcing them through a generic sales presentation. Keep the scope narrow enough that the buyer can correct it.

For example, connect the artifact to task completion, usability observations, and design decisions. Identify which inputs came from the buyer, which are public observations, and which remain assumptions. Do not request private operational records before the buyer has agreed an appropriate purpose and handling process.

Evaluate the result

Track confirmed fit criteria demonstrated. Define the numerator, denominator, and review window before comparing results. Use comparable accounts and channels; otherwise an apparent improvement may simply reflect a different list or buying context.

For product design services, pair the workflow measure with the quality of evidence about a user workflow remaining unclear after feature discussions. A reply or meeting is useful only if it helps establish relevance. Record declined and unsuitable opportunities so the team can improve targeting as well as messaging.

A common failure to avoid

Do not hide an implementation dependency behind a polished screen. Distinguish working capability from a proposed workaround.

In this context, the practical consequence is spending time on a review of one workflow before estimating design work before the buyer has confirmed a reason to do it. Revisit user access, prototype scope, and engineering dependencies early enough to discover a genuine mismatch.

When to move forward

Proceed when the buyer agrees the demonstrated task fits and remaining gaps are explicitly recorded. A sensible next step may be a review of one workflow before estimating design work, but only when it follows from the evidence. If the buyer cannot identify a relevant problem or owner, close or reclassify the record instead of manufacturing urgency.
