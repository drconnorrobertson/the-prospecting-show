Prepare a Podcast Guest Fact Sheet Before the Interview

A useful podcast guest fact sheet gives the host enough verified context to ask clear questions without turning the interview into a recital of an unexamined biography. It identifies the guest, distinguishes current facts from older descriptions and marks claims that need attribution or clarification. The sheet should be short enough to use during the conversation and traceable enough that a producer can check what the introduction actually says.

This guide develops a fictional guest-preparation exercise for The Prospecting Show audience. It is original editorial material, not a description of the show's actual internal production policy or an invented account of a real guest. The example does not establish recording permission, release terms or editorial approval rights. Those matters need the production's actual process. The task here is factual preparation: what you know, where it came from and what remains a question for the interview.

Give the sheet one clear purpose

The sheet should help the host introduce the guest accurately and choose a useful opening question. It is not a complete dossier, a public profile or a script the guest must follow. Keeping that purpose narrow prevents a preparation document from collecting unrelated personal information or burying the important facts under pages of copied search results. A host needs a reliable starting point more than a large pile of plausible details.

Write the planned interview topic at the top. A guest may have several roles, and the relevant introduction depends on why they are appearing. Someone discussing a business transition may need a different description from the same person discussing a community project. Do not assume the most prominent title in a search result is the best framing for this conversation. Match the factual context to the topic without embellishing either.

Record the planned interview date separately from the date the sheet was researched. A role that was current when a profile was published may have changed by recording day. The sheet does not need to imply that every fact expires immediately. It should make the timing visible so the producer can identify which statements are especially worth reconfirming before the host reads them aloud.

Confirm identity through appropriate first-party sources

Begin with the guest's stated name and the identity needed to distinguish them from people with similar names. Use the guest's official site, an authorized organizational profile or direct confirmation through the production's normal contact. A search result with a matching name is not sufficient on its own. Compare the actual organization, role and context before attaching a biography or achievement to the person you intend to interview.

Ask how the guest wants their name pronounced and which form of their name they use publicly. Record the answer as a production note rather than inventing a phonetic guide from appearance alone. If a credential or professional title matters to the introduction, verify the relevant current source rather than assuming that a social-media display name establishes it. Do not add an honorific merely because it makes the introduction sound more authoritative.

Keep personal contact details out of the public-facing fact summary unless there is a specific approved reason to include them. Production may need a scheduling contact, but the host's on-air introduction usually does not. Store logistical details through the actual production process. A fact sheet can remain useful with a source link and an internal contact reference rather than exposing private phone numbers or unrelated personal information.

Separate current role from career history

Use distinct fields for current role and relevant past role. “Founded a company” does not necessarily mean “currently runs the company,” and a former position should not quietly become a present title. Read the dates and wording of the source. If the guest's own biography and an organization page differ, preserve the discrepancy as a question rather than selecting the description that makes the introduction sound strongest.

For the fictional exercise, guest G01's personal page describes them as the founder of an unnamed service company. A dated company announcement says they moved into an advisory role. The fact sheet should not introduce G01 as the current operating chief without clarification. A neutral preparation note can say that the founder description is supported while the current operating role needs confirmation before the introduction is finalized.

This distinction can also improve the interview. Instead of beginning with an inaccurate title, the host can ask how the guest's responsibilities changed during the transition. The question is grounded in an identified source and leaves room for the guest to explain. Preparation should make the conversation more curious and precise, not lock the host into a narrative that the sources do not actually establish.

Label a claim by what the source supports

For each potentially important claim, record the wording, source and evidence status. A guest's company page is a first-party statement about the company. It can support saying the company reports a figure, but it does not automatically provide independent verification of that figure. A trade article may repeat the same statement rather than corroborate it separately. Trace the origin before calling several repeated mentions multiple independent sources.

Use simple evidence labels such as first-party statement, independently documented, historical source or unresolved. Define them for your production team rather than treating the labels as universal certifications. A source can be strong for one fact and weak for another. An official organizational page may establish the published role title while saying little about whether a performance claim was independently measured.

The Society of Professional Journalists' ethics guidance emphasizes verification, context and clear sourcing. Those principles are useful background for preparation, but they do not endorse this particular worksheet or turn it into a legal standard. The practical adaptation here is to make the source and its limits visible. The host can then decide whether to state a fact directly, attribute a claim or ask a clarifying question instead.

Be precise with numbers and time periods

If a guest biography contains a number, identify what is being counted and over what period. Revenue, sales volume, funds raised, customers served and transactions advised are different measures. A host should not substitute one for another because they sound similarly impressive. Keep the unit, date range and relevant qualifier attached to the statement. If the source does not define the measure, the sheet should say so.

In a fictional example, G01's page says the organization served more than two hundred projects over several years. That does not support saying it has two hundred current customers or completed two hundred acquisitions. The fact sheet can quote a short supported description internally and draft an attributed question: “Your site describes work across more than two hundred projects. What kinds of projects are included?” The number remains the guest's reported claim until better evidence supports another description.

Avoid turning a rounded or approximate figure into false precision. If the source says about twenty staff, do not introduce the guest as leading exactly twenty employees without checking the current meaning. Contractors, employees and locations may be counted differently. A concise introduction can omit a number that is not necessary to the topic. Leaving out an unclear boast is often better than spending the opening minutes correcting it.

Build an introduction from verified essentials

Draft an introduction with the guest's confirmed name, relevant role and the topic the conversation will explore. Keep it proportionate. A sentence explaining why the guest is here can be more useful than a string of superlatives. Avoid labels such as leading, top or most successful unless there is a defined, relevant source and a reason the comparison matters. Promotional language should not disguise itself as an independently established fact.

For fictional G01, a draft might read: “Today's guest founded a service company and now advises its team during a leadership transition. We will discuss how responsibilities changed and what the team needed to learn.” This is usable only after those facts are actually confirmed in the exercise. If the advisory role remains unresolved, do not include it as settled. Use a narrower introduction or ask the producer to obtain the missing confirmation.

Read the introduction aloud before finalizing it. Check whether its wording implies more than the sources support. “Built and sold” suggests a completed transaction; “explored a sale” does not. “Works with” differs from “owns.” A short introduction can still contain a consequential error. The fact sheet should help the host hear those distinctions before the recording rather than rely on the guest to correct a confident opening.

Turn research into questions rather than conclusions

For each important source, write a question that invites explanation. If an announcement describes a role change, ask what responsibilities moved and what remained. If a first-party page reports a large number, ask how the measure is defined and what it leaves out. If the biography describes a lesson, ask for a concrete example and the limits of applying it elsewhere. These questions make the research useful without assuming the guest's answer.

Avoid questions that contain an unsupported success story. “How did your system double profits?” asserts the result before the guest has explained it. A more grounded question might ask what changed, how it was measured and what else was happening at the time. The host can explore a claim without treating it as independently proven. That distinction matters especially when the conversation may later be summarized in a headline or social clip.

Prepare follow-ups for uncertainty. “What do you know directly?” “Which period does that cover?” and “What would change your interpretation?” can be useful in the right context. They are examples, not a script to repeat mechanically. Listen to the actual answer and choose the question that clarifies it. The fact sheet should support attention during the conversation, not compete with the guest through a rigid sequence of prewritten prompts.

Separate factual confirmation from editorial control

A producer may ask a guest to confirm their name, title, pronunciation or a source discrepancy before recording. That factual check is different from promising approval of the final episode, every question or every promotional line. Follow the show's actual policy and any applicable agreement. This guide does not create a review right or advise a production to make a promise it has not decided to offer.

Make any requested correction specific. “Is this your current role title?” is clearer than “Please approve the whole interview.” If the guest supplies a revised fact, record what changed and the source of the update. If the change concerns a disputed claim rather than a straightforward identity detail, the production may need more review. A guest's preference and an independently supportable fact are not always the same thing.

Likewise, resolve expectations about recording, sensitive topics and logistics through the actual production process before relying on them. Do not assume that a fact sheet substitutes for permission or that a public biography makes every personal topic appropriate. The sheet can flag a question for the producer. It should not silently decide legal or ethical boundaries on behalf of the host, guest and production team.

Work through the fictional source discrepancy

The fictional preparation file contains three sources: G01's own biography, a company role-change announcement and an older event profile. The biography uses founder, the announcement describes an advisory role and the event profile still says chief executive. The producer records the event profile as historical rather than current. They ask through the normal contact which role description is accurate for the planned recording date.

Suppose the fictional response confirms that G01 remains founder and adviser but no longer manages daily operations. The introduction can now use that confirmed description, with the response date retained in the working file. The host's first question can explore what daily decisions moved to the new operator. The research has produced a better question and prevented an outdated title from becoming an on-air claim.

Now suppose the response does not arrive before the planned recording. The sheet should not mark the current role confirmed because the producer expects the answer. Use a supported narrower description and keep the unresolved issue visible to the host. A live clarification may be appropriate under the production's normal approach. The example shows why an unanswered question should remain a question rather than being promoted to fact by the passage of time.

Keep research notes separate from publication copy

A working fact sheet may include uncertainty, conflicting sources and internal questions that do not belong in a public episode description. Do not paste it directly into show notes. Publication copy needs its own review against what was actually said and the evidence available. The Prospecting Show's separate traceable-notes guide addresses the post-interview task of connecting statements and source references to the final material.

Similarly, a pre-interview assumption can become outdated during the conversation. If the guest clarifies a date or explains that a reported metric includes a different population, update the relevant production note. Preserve the distinction between the earlier source and the new statement. Do not make the recording appear to confirm a number the guest actually qualified or disputed. The final description should reflect the actual conversation, not the more convenient preparation narrative.

Keep internal comments clearly marked. A note such as ask whether this is current should not accidentally appear in an introduction sent to the host as final copy. Use a small approved-introduction section and a separate unresolved-questions section. This is an editorial organization choice, not a substitute for the production's normal review. Its purpose is to reduce confusion about which words are ready to be read and which are still working notes.

Make the sheet usable during the conversation

Limit the on-air reference section to the facts and questions the host is likely to need. Put source details and longer background in a separate area that a producer can inspect. A host trying to read several pages while listening may miss the answer that deserves a follow-up. The fact sheet should make the next relevant detail easy to find, not reward the amount of research copied into it.

Use clear labels and consistent identifiers. A claim reference such as C03 can point to a source entry without placing a long URL in the host's speaking notes. Keep the full link accessible in the working file. Mark pronunciation and current-role confirmation near the introduction. Mark unresolved numerical claims near the related question. The structure should follow how the host and producer actually use the material.

After a rehearsal or recording, ask which parts of the sheet helped and which created distraction. Remove redundant biography paragraphs and keep questions that led to a clearer explanation. This does not mean making every episode follow the same template. It means improving the preparation aid so it supports the conversation's purpose. A concise, accurate sheet can leave more room for listening than a polished but overloaded research packet.

Review the final introduction immediately before recording

Read the confirmed essentials again, especially time-sensitive role descriptions and the planned topic. Check that the host has the final version rather than an earlier draft. If a late correction arrives, make the change visible and remove the superseded introduction from the immediate speaking reference. A small version distinction can prevent a producer's correction from remaining hidden in an email while the host reads the old wording.

Do not turn this review into a search for more impressive claims. The introduction's job is to orient the audience accurately. If a number remains unclear and is not necessary, omit it or frame it as a question in the conversation. If a title remains unresolved, use the supported description. The interview can still be interesting without a promotional opening that exceeds the evidence.

A good guest fact sheet leaves the host with a clear identity, a relevant topic and several informed questions. It also leaves uncertainty visible where the preparation did not resolve it. That combination supports a conversation in which the guest can explain their experience and the audience can understand the context. The document earns its usefulness through accuracy and restraint, not by making every guest sound like a proven universal authority.

Frequently asked questions

Should the guest's own biography be treated as independently verified?

No. It is a first-party source that can be useful for identity and stated experience, but its claims still need appropriate attribution and review. Several articles repeating the same biography may not add independent corroboration. Record where the statement originated and what the source actually supports. If a claim matters to the introduction or promotional copy, resolve the relevant uncertainty rather than assuming repetition establishes it.

Do I need to include every achievement in the introduction?

No. Select the confirmed facts relevant to the planned conversation. A concise introduction can orient the audience without reproducing a full career history or unclear performance claim. Omit superlatives and numbers that are unnecessary or insufficiently supported. The fact sheet can retain background for questions while the spoken opening remains accurate, focused and proportionate to the topic.

Can a factual check promise the guest final approval?

Not unless the production has actually chosen and agreed to that process. Confirming a name or title is different from granting editorial review of an episode or promotional material. Follow the actual policy and agreement. This guide does not create approval rights, recording permission or legal terms. Use precise language about the specific confirmation requested so expectations remain clear.

What if public sources disagree about the current role?

Record the dates and wording, identify which source is historical and seek clarification through the normal production contact. Until the issue is resolved, use a narrower supported description or leave a clear question for the host. Do not choose the most impressive title merely because it makes the opening easier to write. A discrepancy is useful preparation information, not a fact to hide.

How is this different from preparing show notes?

The fact sheet supports the conversation before recording. Show notes describe and source material from the actual episode afterward. A preparation assumption may change when the guest speaks, so the two documents should not be interchangeable. Use the separate traceable-notes workflow for the post-interview task and review final publication copy against what the recording and sources actually support.

Does the worksheet establish recording consent or release terms?

No. It is an editorial preparation aid. Recording permission, release terms, sensitive-topic handling and review rights require the production's actual process and relevant advice where needed. The sheet can flag an unresolved question for the producer, but it should not imply those matters are settled merely because a guest's name and biography have been researched.
